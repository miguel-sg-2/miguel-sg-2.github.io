About me

I am a researcher in the Morton K. Blaustein Department of Earth & Planetary Sciences at Johns Hopkins University. I study how multiscale boundary-layer processes shape extreme-weather hazards, particularly tropical cyclones, and how those hazards translate into the environmental conditions experienced by energy infrastructure. I combine high-fidelity numerical modeling with physics-informed machine learning to understand and predict extreme winds and turbulence across scales, with the goal of improving hazard prediction, infrastructure resilience, and preparedness.

My background is in mechanical engineering, which first drew me to problems at the intersection of environmental flows and wind energy systems. During my PhD in Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences at the University of Colorado Boulder, I focused on multiscale modeling of the atmospheric boundary layer and its applications to wind energy. I then joined the National Wind Technology Center at NREL, now the National Laboratory of the Rockies, first as a postdoctoral researcher and later as a Research Engineer III. There, my work increasingly focused on extreme weather, particularly tropical cyclones, and on translating high-fidelity atmospheric simulations into wind conditions and structural loads relevant to turbine design. I now build on that foundation at Johns Hopkins, where I am expanding this work toward broader questions of extreme-weather hazards, infrastructure response, and resilience.

Research

Multiscale boundary-layer physics in extreme weather

Storm circulations span hundreds of kilometers while the turbulence that matters to a structure is meters across. I use multiscale numerical simulations, coupling regional-scale and turbulence-resolving models, to understand the atmospheric processes that govern extreme winds and turbulence across scales and environments. This resolves near-surface winds and turbulence with a fidelity that conventional engineering representations cannot provide.

Related papers: J. Geophys. Res. Atmos. 2023, J. Atmos. Sci. 2025, Q. J. R. Meteorol. Soc. 2025

Schematic: Multiscale simulation of Hurricane Laura showing complex near-surface turbulence at infrastructure scales that are not captured by conventional engineering wind models.

From realistic atmospheric hazards to structural and system-level response

Realistic flow fields from multiscale simulations can drive structural and system simulations, revealing environmental conditions and interactions that conventional engineering representations miss. I use turbulence-resolving simulations as inflow to aeroelastic load calculations to find out whether high-fidelity conditions produce loads outside design envelopes, and which properties of the environment are responsible.

Related papers: J. Phys. Conf. Ser. 2026, plus two manuscripts submitted to the Journal of Wind Engineering and Industrial Aerodynamics (see publications)

Schematic: High-fidelity multiscale simulation of a hurricane drives aeroelastic wind turbine simulations to estimate the structural loads during the hurricane passage.

Physics-informed AI for prediction, risk assessment and preparedness

High-fidelity simulations are too expensive to run for every storm and every site. I develop physics-informed AI methods that bridge the gap between those simulations and the representations of extreme weather needed for infrastructure design, hazard prediction, risk assessment and preparedness. For example, diffusion models that reconstruct coherent turbulence from coarse simulations. I also work with design communities to translate these insights into practical applications.

Related papers: Environ. Res. Commun. 2025, preprint under review at J. Adv. Model. Earth Syst.
